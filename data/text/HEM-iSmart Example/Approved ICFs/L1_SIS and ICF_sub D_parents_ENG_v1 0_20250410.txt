CHAPTER I – DESCRIPTION OF THE TRIAL AND YOUR CHILD’s RIGHTS WHEN PARTICIPATING

Why are we doing this trial

Your child has relapsed or refractory leukaemia or lymphoma. As your child’s treating physician has informed you and your child, there is no standard therapy available for your child’s disease. Treatment of this disease remains an important topic of investigation, which is why the HEM-iSMART trial was designed. This clinical trial (further on referred to as “trial”) aims to identify new combinations of agents for children and adolescents suffering from relapsed or refractory leukaemia or lymphoma (R/R ALL an LBL) and to define the safety and efficacy of those new combinations. The goal is to improve the outcome / increase the long-term survival of children and adolescents with this disease.

Leukaemia or lymphoma cells may be subject to changes (mutations) that cause cancer cells to develop more quickly and keep growing afterwards. The changes that cause this effect can be measured in or on cells, or can be observed due to changes to a cell’s task. These indicators are called molecular or functional biomarkers.The trial aims to create a sustainable international platform that can help allocate patients to treatment options based on a harmonized interpretation of those molecular and functional biomarkers. So far, we have developed three different treatment options (sub-protocols). Depending on the mutations that your child’s leukaemia or lymphoma cells have, your child is allocated to one of those treatment options.

Leukaemia or lymphoma cells may be subject to changes (mutations) that cause leukaemia cells to develop more quickly and keep growing. Depending on the mutations that your child’s leukaemia or lymphoma cells have, your child is allocated to one of the three treatment options. Known changes that have such an effect are mutations in different genes known as the ‘RAS signalling pathway’. In the rest of this letter, we will refer to these mutations as ‘RAS mutations’. Patients who have been diagnosed with a RAS mutation tend to be harder to treat than those without such mutations. RAS mutations have been found in your child’s leukaemia cells.Trametinib is a medication that blocks these RAS mutations and, as a result, may slow down the growth of cancer cells. Dexamethasone, cyclophosphamide and cytarabine are medications that may break down leukaemia and lymphoma cells.

Trametinib has previously been administered to patients suffering from various types of cancer: a certain type of lung cancer, skin cancer and thyroid cancer. So far, nearly 2,000 people have been administered this medication. Trametinib has not yet been approved for use in children, but has previously been tested in children with other forms of cancer, and in adults with leukaemia and other types of cancer. Those studies showed that trametinib can be administered in combination with chemotherapy, and also that it can be safely administered to children.

Dexamethasone, cyclophosphamide and cytarabine are medications that are used in the standard treatment of acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) and lymphoblastic lymphoma (LL). Your child has probably been administered these medications before. However, this particular combination of these medications has never been investigated before, in children or adult patients.

Laboratory trials in ALL cells have shown that the combination of trametinib and dexamethasone is more effective than either trametinib or dexamethasone alone, since the two medications enhance each other’s effect.

If it is found that the combination of these medications is safe, the investigators will proceed to Phase 2, in which they will focus on whether the combination is effective in treating the disease. We hope that trametinib will fight off your child’s disease to such an extent that he/she/they will be able to undergo a stem cell transplant. If your child is eligible for one, the doctor will discuss this with you and your child.

Why is your child being asked to take part?

Your child has been diagnosed with relapsed or refractory leukaemia or lymphoma. You are being asked for your child to take part in this trial because it is believed that he/she/thyis potentially a suitable candidate.

Your childhas relapsed or refractory leukaemia or lymphoma and for this condition the standard treatments aiming at improving survival have not worked. For your child’s relapsed or refractory leukaemia or lymphoma, no treatment is currently available in Belgium that has been approved by the authorities. It cannot be guaranteed that your child’s participation in this trial will cure his/her/their disease, improve the quality of life, or extend your child’s life.

The investigator or trial staff will discuss with you the requirements for your child to be allowed to enter the trial.

Does your child has to take part in a trial?

Your child’s participation in a trial is voluntary and must remain free of any coercion. This means that you have the right for your child not to take part in the trial or to withdraw at any time without giving a reason, even if you previously agreed for your child to take part. This decision will not affect your relationship with the investigator or your child’s treating physician nor will it affect the quality of your future medical care.

If other treatments are available for relapsed or refractory leukaemia or lymphoma, the investigator or his/her delegate will discuss these treatments with you and your child.

What will happen during the trial? 

Taking into account the three combinations of new agents (depending on the mutations that are found), this HEM-iSMART trial will include about approximately 69 participants worldwide, including about 4 participants in Belgium.

This specific trial (sub-protocol D) is for patients for whom mutations have been found in the RAS signalling pathway’. This specific trial will include about 26 participants in various European countries, including about 1 or 2 participants in Belgium.

This trial consists of two phases: Phase 1 and Phase 2. Your child’s doctor will be able to tell you which phase the trial is in currently. The purpose of Phase 1 is to ascertain the dose of the medication, Trametinib, combined with dexamethasone, cyclophosphamide and cytarabine, that is safe in patients whose symptoms did not properly respond to previous courses of treatment (i.e. who had refractory symptoms) or who have experienced a relapse (return of the disease). In Phase 1 of this trial, we will investigate two different doses of the medication. We will provide more information later in this letter. Your child’s doctor will be able to tell you which dose is currently being investigated.

If it is found that the combination of these medications is safe, the investigators will proceed to Phase 2, in which they will focus on whether the combination is effective in treating the disease.

Pharmaceutical company Novartis will make the medication used in the trial available to the subjects free of charge.

How long will the trial last?

Is your child taking part in the trial? If so, depending on the number of cycles your child will undergo, the course of treatment with Trametinib will last between 1 and 6 months. If your child’s leukaemia or lymphoma responds well to the course of treatment, the therapy may be extended at the end of the trial. Once the course of treatment has been completed, we would like to keep monitoring your child, until 1 year after the start of the therapy.

Step 1: Is your child a suitable candidate for this trial?

The first thing we wish to determine is whether your child is a suitable candidate for the trial. We will do so during the screening period. The investigator will perform several examinations to determine your child’s eligibility. For instance, a physical examination, a thorough look at your child’s medical history, a blood test, a bone marrow aspirate, a lumbar puncture, a urinalysis, an X-ray (or CT scan) of the lungs, an electrocardiogram, an echocardiogram, a pregnancy test (if relevant), and a questionnaire and an eye examination. Furthermore, we will discuss any other medication(s) your child may be taking.

In Appendix A you will find a list of all the examinations to be performed. Most of these tests are also performed in the standard therapy, and we will be collecting the results of these tests as part of this trial. Some of the tests are not part of the standard therapy and are specifically performed as part of the trial. Tests in this category can be identified in the overview by means of an ‘*’.

Once all these examinations have been performed, the investigator will discuss the results with you and your child and tell you whether your child is eligible for the Trametinib trial. Your child will only be allowed to take part in the trial if he/she/they meet all the inclusion criteria. If the screening results are such that your child will not be allowed to take part, the treating physician will discuss any available alternatives with you.

The investigator will send your child’s general practitioner a letter to inform him/her that your child is taking part in the trial. This is done for your child’s safety.

Step 2: the study treatment

In this trial, your child will be administered Trametinib combined with standard chemotherapy. He/she/they will receive this combination of medications for 1 to 6 months. At the start of each new cycle, we will have another look at the right dose for your child, based on the age and body weight.

Trametinib

Trametinib comes as a tablet formulation. At the start of each new cycle, we will have another look at the right dose for your child, based on your age and body weight. Your child will have to take this medication once daily. Twenty-four hours must pass between each administration of the medication. More information on taking the medication can be found in the medication diary, in which you and your child will record daily when you take the medication.

The amount and/or frequency of the dexamethasone, cyclophosphamide and cytarabine to be given to your child will depend on the dosage being investigated. There will be two different dosages: Dose Level 1 and Dose Level -1. We will start the trial by administering the highest dose level (Dose Level 1). We will use the same dose of trametinib in both dose levels. If Dose Level 1 is not well tolerated, we will reduce the amount of dexamethasone administered in Dose Level -1.

At the end of the first 28-day cycle, we will evaluate whether the course of treatment is sufficiently effective with regard to your child’s disease and whether he/she/they is fit enough to undergo another cycle. If this is the case, your child will be started on Cycle 2.

Dose Level 1: In Cycle 1, your child will be administered trametinib once daily for 28 days, in combination with dexamethasone and standard chemotherapy.

In all cycles, your child will receive dexamethasone for the first five days. Dexamethasone is ingested in tablet form or intravenously. In other words, it can either be taken at home or be administered in the hospital.

In Cycle 1, your child will be intravenously administered cyclophosphamide on Day 3, and cytarabine on Days 5-8 and on Days 12-15. In Cycle 2, your child will be intravenously administered cyclophosphamide on Day 1, and cytarabine on Days 3-6 and on Days 10-13. If your child feels good enough, the medication can be given as a day treatment, not requiring an overnight stay. If not, he/she/they will be admitted to hospital for the administration of the medication. At the start of each cycle, your child will probably need to spend a few days in hospital.

More information on this is provided below.

Step 3: examinations and tests

As part of this trial, your child will have to visit the hospital every week if he/she/they is not actually staying overnight at the hospital.

We will perform the following examinations:

- Physical examination. For instance, the investigator will listen to your child’s heart and lungs and measure your blood pressure.

- Blood test. Your child’s blood will be collected for testing purposes. Two 2ml tubes of blood will be taken each time.

- Review of your child’s patient diary, which your child has to fill in daily.

- Urinalysis

- On Day 14 of Cycle 1, blood will be collected several times to determine the medicine blood level and 15-20 drops of cerebrospinal fluid will be collected once. This is called a pharmacokinetic assay. When we perform a pharmacokinetic assay, we analyse the amount of medication in the body at various points after administration of the medication. In this way, we seek to determine whether we are administering the right dose. We will collect blood once before trametinib dosing, and then three more times, at 4, 6 and 8 hours after trametinib dosing.

At the end of each 28-day trametinib cycle, additional tests will be performed to determine if the course of treatment has been effective:

- You and your child will be asked to complete a questionnaire (only at the start and end of Cycle 1). The questions will be about your and your child’s quality of life.

- Pregnancy test for girls who can become pregnant.

- Bone marrow examination

- A scan (e.g. a CT scan), only if the leukaemia or lymphoma has spread to other parts of the body

- Cerebrospinal fluid analysis

- Blood test

- Urinalysis

- Electrocardiogram (ECG) and echocardiogram

- Eye examination (if necessary)

Bone marrow can be found in the innermost part of our bones. This is where blood is produced. Your child will be sedated when the bone marrow aspirate is performed, so he/she/they will not feel a thing. You and your child will receive information on the examination and the sedative beforehand. A tiny needle is used to suction a small amount of bone marrow (aspirate) from the pelvis. Sometimes (in rare cases) a small piece of bone (= biopsy) will be collected, as well. The bone marrow and the piece of bone will be examined in a laboratory. A plaster will be affixed to the spot where the procedure is performed.

Appendix A lists the procedures to be carried out each time your child visits the hospital.

We would like to perform one last check-up 30 days after the final dose of study medication is administered. We would also like to keep monitoring your child, until 1 year after the start of the course of treatment. As part of the monitoring procedure, your child will have to come to hospital about four times. The monitoring appointments will coincide with regular follow-up appointments. During these appointments, we will physically examine your child and check how he/she/they is doing.

Overall, your child’s participation in the trial will last between 1 and 6 months – depending on your child’s health status.

If your chlid meets all the conditions required to be enrolled in the trial and agree to take part in the trial, he/she/they will undergo the above-mentioned tests and examinations. Will your child benefit from the trial? 

The information obtained during a trial may contribute to a better understanding of the use of the investigational medicinal product (referred to as “IMP”) or to the development of a new medicinal product for the treatment of your child or future patients.

The IMP may or may not be beneficial in treating your child’s disease or relieving his/her/theirsymptoms. Even if it is beneficial to your child, a potential return or worsening of symptoms, illness or disease is still possible.

What are the possible risks and discomforts of taking part?

Taking part in the trial may be both beneficial and detrimental to your child’s health. We will list the benefits and risks below. We advise you to carefully consider them and discuss them with others.

The study medication may reduce your child’s symptoms, but this is by no means guaranteed. Your child may grow worse at any point during the course of the trial.

By participating, your child are assisting us in the search for better therapies against relapsed or refractory leukaemia or lymphoma. In the best-case scenario, the chemotherapy will prove to be effective, meaning that your child will be able to undergo a stem cell transplant. The investigator will be able to tell you and your child more about the prospects of recovery.

1.  What are the possible side effects of trametinib?

Participation in a trial involves some risk.

Trametinib has not yet been approved for use in children, but has previously been tested in children with other forms of cancer, and in adults with leukaemia and other types of cancer. Those studies showed that trametinib can be administered in combination with chemotherapy, and also that it can be safely administered to children.

Dexamethasone, cyclophosphamide and cytarabine are medications that are used in the standard treatment of acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) and lymphoblastic lymphoma (LL). You have probably been administered these medications before. However, this particular combination of these medications has never been investigated before, in children or adult patients.

The study medications (trametinib, dexamethasone, cyclophosphamide and cytarabine) may cause side effects or adverse effects. Some of these side effects are already known, and some are not known. Even if previous studies have shown that trametinib was normally well tolerated, your child may still experience side effects or adverse effects.

Since your child has leukaemia or lymphoma, he/she/they probably does not have many immune cells – a problem with which you and your child will be familiar from previous courses of treatment.

Contact the investigator at once if your child experiences any of the following adverse effects:

- fever

- feeling ill or extremely tired

- nausea or vomiting

- impaired vision (blurred vision)

For a more complete overview of known side effects, refer to Appendix B.

The study medications may also cause side effects we are currently unaware of. For this reason, your childis requested to record any symptoms he/she/they may experience in the diary. Please call the investigator or clinical research nurse if your childexperiences any symptoms that are new or serious, or if you or your child have any concerns.

Because this combination of medication is still under investigation, other currently unknown risks and discomforts could occur. Therefore, it is very important that you report for your child any new or worsened health problems immediately to the investigator, regardless of whether or not you think it has to do with the trial medication and even when it is already described in this document. If your child needs to use other medication, discuss this with the investigator beforehand. If, for any reason, your child consults another treating physician during the trial you must inform him/her that your child is taking part in a trial and present your child’s emergency card. This could be important in determining a diagnosis and giving your childthe correct treatment if needed.

 Which biological samples are collected from my child during the trial and what will happen with them? 

1.  Which biological samples are collected from my child during the trial?

Biological samples are samples of human body material (for example blood, tissue, urine, faecal stool, ….)

In this trial, the following biological sample(s) will be taken: blood, urine, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and bone marrow.

The procedure to encode your child’s biological samples is the same as that used for his / her personal data in accordance with Belgian legislation on the use of human body material (see I § 12.2). Samples sent to the sponsor or to organisations working in collaboration with the sponsor, will only be labelled with your child’s trial identification code. The sponsor ensures that the location of your child’s biological samples is always tracked. The use of your child’s biological samples is linked to the processing of the associated coded personal data.

Your child’s biological samples are deemed to be a “donation”. Nor you nor your child will not receive any financial benefit associated with the development of new therapies derived from the use of your child’s biological samples, and which may have commercial value.

2.  What will happen to the collected biological samples?

The collected biological samples will be managed and stored for 25 years at various laboratories inside and outside the hospital. The tubes of blood and cerebrospinal fluid collected for the pharmacokinetic assay(s) will be sent to the National Cancer Institute (NKI) in Amsterdam, the Netherlands, for pharmacokinetic testing.

The bone marrow sample will be sent to the Charité Hospital in Berlin, Germany, where your child’s reaction to the course of treatment will be assessed. This is called a centralised review.

These biological samples will be analysed for the objectives of the trial.

Since scientific progress in this area is constant, the sponsor would like to, with your and your child’s consent retain the remainders of your child’s biological samples for 25 years. The sponsor will use them for additional research that remains within the context of the current clinical trial and thus with the same disease or the same treatment/drug as in this study.

You agree or disagree to the retention of the remainder of your child’s biological samples for additional research within the context of the current clinical trial by ticking the appropriate checkbox in Chapter II.

The residuary samples (collected for standard testing purposes) will be stored for 25 years in the Biobank Pediatrische Hematologie-Oncologie en Stamceltransplantatie UZ Gent, Belgium.

It may happen by chance and in addition to the trial objectives, that the results of the analysis of your child’s biological samples reveal information that may be important to your child’s health or the health of your blood relatives. These data are called ”incidental findings” and will be treated as described in Chapter I, § 15.

3.  Will any additional biological samples be collected and used for additional research?

In this trial, no additional biological samples will be collected.

Appendix B

Potential side effects, adverse effects and discomforts associated with trametinib

Very common (at least 1 in 10 patients may experience these side effects):

- High blood pressure

- Bleeding (external bleeding, pain, swelling, nausea and vomiting)

- Coughing

- Chest tightness

- Diarrhoea

- Nausea

- Vomiting

- Constipation

- Abdominal pain

- Dry mouth

- Skin rash

- Some type of acne

- Dry skin

- Itching

- Hair loss

- Fatigue

- Swelling of hands or feet

- Fever

- Abnormal liver function, which may mean that the liver is not functioning properly; such abnormalities tend to be mild and reversible but can be severe or life-threatening. Your liver function will be checked regularly during the course of the trial. Please notify your child’s treating physician or clinical research nurse if you notice any of the following changes on your child: itching, skin turning yellow, whites of the eyes turning yellow, dark urine, pain or an uncomfortable sensation in the upper right abdomen.

Common (1 in 100 up to 1 in 10 patients may experience these side effects):

- Inflammation of the hair follicles

- Inflammation of the cuticles

- Skin effects: thickening of the skin, skin fissures, warts, small and benign skin growths, small wounds, flaky or scaly patches of skin that may be an early sign of skin cancer, redness, small wounds oozing pus, and skin rash, including an acne-like rash.

- Inflammation of the subcutaneous fat tissue, with red and painful bumps in the skin

- Cellulitis

- Anaemia

- Hypersensitivity (immune system abnormalities)

- Dehydration

- Blurred vision

- Fluid retention around the eyes or eyelids

- Impaired vision

- Impaired cardiac function

- Reduced heart rate

- Pneumonia

- Stomatitis (inflammation of the oral mucosa)

- Redness

- Skin rash in the palm of the hand and sole of the foot

- Skin fissures

- Cracked skin

- Facial swelling

- Mucosal inflammation

- Feeling weak, or lack of energy

- Abnormal muscle protein values (elevated blood creatine phosphokinase levels)

Infrequent side effects (fewer than 1 in 100 patients may experience these side effects):

- Eye-related problems, including an occluded or bleeding retinal vein, inflammation of the eye, swelling around the eyes, retinal detachment and other retinal disorders, which may cause the patient to experience blurred vision. In many cases, these vision-related problems will pass, but in some cases they will not, and may result in blindness.

- Heart failure, causing the heart to be unable to pump blood around the body properly. This may result in fatigue, swelling and shortness of breath.

  - Retinal detachment in the eye

  - Lymphoedema

  - Pneumonia

  - Intestinal perforation (stomach ache, nausea, vomiting and fever)

  - Colitis (diarrhoea, often involving blood and slime, sometimes involving pus, stomach ache, stomach cramps and abdominal swelling).

  - Excessive muscle atrophy (pain, swelling, haphalgesia (pain upon physical contact) or sensitivity at the level of the affected area, loss of consciousness, confusion and dehydration, possibly with fever, paralysis or dysaesthesia of the limbs, dark urine, very little to no urine production).

Appendix C

If your child isa girl and of childbearing age, it is vital that your child does not fall pregnant during the course of the trial and for at least 6 months after the last dose of chemotherapy. In addition, your child should not have any egg cells stored (either for her own use or for donation) while your child is taking part in the trial and for 6 months after the last dose of chemotherapy. You child must not breastfeed any infants during the trial and for at least 35 days after the last dose of chemotherapy.

If your child is a boy and is taking part in the trial, he must not cause a pregnancy or donate sperm during the trial and for 6 months after the last dose of chemotherapy.

If your child is capable of becoming pregnant and is heterosexually active, or if your child has a partner who may become pregnant, the treating physician or nurse will explain the different types of contraceptives that your child must use during and after the trial. Approved forms of contraception include:

- Hormonal contraceptives that are taken by mouth, inserted into the vagina, applied to the skin in the form of a skin patch, injected or implanted under the skin

- A coil (IUD) (some IUDs contain hormones)

- Refraining from having sex (complete sexual abstinence)

If your child becomes pregnant during this trial, or if your child’s partner becomes pregnant during this trial, you must notify the investigator or the members of the study team at once. If your child falls pregnant, the treating physician will discuss with you and your child how this will impact your child’s course of treatment. The investigator will inform the sponsor and the Medical Ethics Review Committee that monitors this trial of the pregnancy and the outcome of the pregnancy.
