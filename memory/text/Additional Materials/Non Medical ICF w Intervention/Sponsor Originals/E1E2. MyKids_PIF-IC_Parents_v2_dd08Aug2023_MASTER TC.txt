1.  General information

This study is set up as part of the ‘European Paediatric Soft tissue sarcoma Study Group’, abbreviated to ‘EpSSG’. This is a European collaboration of doctors and investigators who are actively working towards an improved treatment of children with a soft-tissue tumour. The study is internationally coordinated by the Princess Máxima Center (hereafter referred to as the Sponsor). The study will be performed by doctors in different hospitals in Europe and a number of countries outside Europe. In <country>, the study will be performed in <number> hospitals .

We expect that 250 children from different countries will take part in this study. In <country> we expect <number> children to take part. The <name local MREC / IRB> medical ethics committee has approved this study.

2.  What is the purpose of the study?

The purpose of this study is to learn more about the origin and behaviour of rare soft-tissue tumours in children and young adults. We want to use this to improve the treatment of patients with a soft-tissue tumour in the future.

3.  What is the background to the study?

Your child has been diagnosed with a soft-tissue tumour or there is a suspicion of a soft-tissue tumour. Soft tissues are, for example, the muscles, connective tissue, fatty tissue or the walls of blood vessels. Soft-tissue tumours can appear anywhere in the body. About half of the tumours in the soft tissues in children and young adults is a ‘rhabdomyosarcoma’. The other half consists of different, other, rare soft-tissue tumours. This study concerns this other half of soft-tissue tumours, the so-called 'non-rhabdomyosarcoma soft-tissue sarcomas (NRSTS)'.

Little is known about the origin of soft-tissue tumours, especially about the group with different types of rare soft-tissue tumours.

The origin of many forms of cancer (including paediatric) lies in the tumour’s genetic material. This is also called its molecular profile. For these reasons, more and more medicines are being developed that specifically target the particular aberrations in the genetic material (DNA) of tumour cells or in the material that the tumour cell needs to produce proteins (RNA).

In this study we want to examine these rare tumours thoroughly in the laboratory. Various components will be examined:

A.  The genetic material in your child’s tumour cells will be mapped fully. We call this the ‘molecular profile’. This information can help with confirming the diagnosis and understanding how this kind of soft-tissue tumour develop.

B.  To diagnose a soft-tissue tumour, the pathologist examines a piece of tissue under a microscope. This can be very complicated for this kind of rare tumour. Within this study European pathology experts will repeat this examination (under the microscope) to check whether the correct diagnosis was made. Factors that predict whether the tumour will grow rapidly or slowly will also be sought. That is still a question for research. Results may thus not yet be included when determining the best treatment for your child.

C.  In this study we shall try to grow the tumour cells in the laboratory. We do this outside the body in a Petri dish. These tumour cells continue growing outside the body to form a mini-organ or mini-tumour called an 'organoid'. We can conduct much further scientific research on these organoids and tumour cells. These organoids and tumour cells can be used for research for a long time, even after your child’s bodily material has been destroyed. These cultivated cells can be frozen, thawed and used for research even far into the future. We hope to learn more about the origin, growth and behaviour of these rare tumours.

D.  In blood samples, we want to look for pieces of the tumour’s genetic material (tumour markers). We call this Liquid Biopsy. In the future we want to use this technique to detect tumours easily in the blood, for example in the phase after concluding the treatment if children undergo regular check-ups in the follow-up phase.

E.  If the tumour is first pre-treated with chemotherapy, an operation is done later to remove the tumour. We would like to examine the molecular profile of tissue from the removed tumour to see if anything has changed during the treatment.

You will be asked to give separate consent for each part of this study. Part B is mandatory to be able to participate in the study. In addition, you can consent to Parts A, C, D and E individually.

All these studies serve to improve our knowledge and understanding of the tumour and its behaviour during treatment. In addition, the molecular examinations may sometimes produce starting points for new medicines that may act on the tumour. In this way, we are trying to improve the chance of curing children with a soft-tissue tumour in the future.

4.  Course of the study

The surgeon will remove the entire tumour or a small piece of tumour tissue (biopsy) from your child under general anaesthesia. This may already have taken place. In that case we shall request a piece of your child’s tumour biopsy from the biobank, if it is stored there. The tissue will be processed, placed under the microscope and evaluated by a pathologist. This results in a diagnosis. This biopsy or operation is standard, it would also be done if you don’t give consent to take part in this study. Once the diagnosis is made, your child will be treated in the standard manner for the disease. The treating doctor will discuss the treatment plan with you and your child.

As described above, we want to examine a piece of tumour tissue for this study. The tumour tissue will be sent to a number of laboratories in Europe for further examination. These laboratories are the hospital laboratory in Centre Léon Bérard in Lyon (France), the Princess Máxima Center in Utrecht (the Netherlands) and in some cases also the Ospedale Pediatrico Bambino Gesù in Rome (Italy).

Along with the use of your child’s bodily material sample, we register the data about the diagnosis, treatment and result of your child’s treatment. We do this from the start of participation in this study, and we continue registering how your child is doing for 4 years afterwards.

The intention is to improve the correct diagnosis of soft-tissue tumours through international re-evaluation under the microscope. In addition, we shall collect the molecular profiles of all soft-tissue tumours from this study so we can rapidly learn more about the molecular profiles of these types of tumours in children.

The European investigators discuss together the outcomes of all the examinations done with your child’s tumour tissue and blood. If there are results that could lead to a better treatment for your child, we shall inform your child’s treating doctor of this. The doctor is likely to adjust the treatment appropriately and discuss the findings with you. It is also possible that this study will not produce any results that would be useful for adjusting your child’s treatment. In that case, your child will not benefit from participating in this study. But his/her participation in this study will contribute to our knowledge about soft-tissue tumours in children and young adults.

For your child, the study will last four years, with the majority of the time consisting of registering data and follow-up treatments. We call this the follow-up phase. In this phase your child does not have to come to the hospital for additional visits, your child comes to the hospital for a check-up as usual once every three or six months. The treating doctor will check your child according to standard of care, no extra examinations or measurements will be done. We use the data from these check-up visits for the study. The entire study will take about eight years in total.

Extra blood sample collections for scientific research (part D)

At a number of time points, we would like to collect extra blood samples for scientific research. With new techniques we can measure tiny amounts of the tumour’s genetic material in the blood. We want to investigate whether this technique could be used for rare soft-tissue tumours as well. We measure this during and after the treatment. We investigate whether this says anything about the result of the treatment and the chances of cure.

For this part of the study, we want to collect about 5-10 ml more blood each time:

- At diagnosis

- During the treatment, when the scans are done. Usually for children, we do this 2 to 4 times during the treatment

- At the end of the treatment

- At the moment that the disease recurs, if it does

We will combine the extra blood sample collections for the study with regular blood sample collections where possible. With most children, we use the central line or the Port-A-Cath (PAC) for access; that’s a catheter positioned in a major vein under your child’s skin. Occasionally, your child may need to be pricked again for these collections.

Participation in this part of the study is not mandatory. You have to consent separately for it. If you do not consent to your child participating in this part of the study, your child can still participate in the other parts of the study.

Hereditary diseases

Within this research it is standard practice to examine the genetic material of healthy cells as well. This allows the tumour tissue to be compared with the healthy cells. For this we also need a small amount of extra blood, about 5-10 ml. This blood sampling can usually be combined with a blood sample done for standard of care procedures.

If a hereditary condition is found that has no direct influence on your child's treatment, you can indicate separately on the consent form whether you want to be informed about it. If a hereditary condition is found that does affect your child's medical care, your child's treating doctor will always discuss this with you. If desired, the treating doctor will make an appointment for you with a doctor who specializes in hereditary diseases for further explanation and guidance.

Information about hereditary conditions can be burdensome for you and your family. If you find it difficult to decide whether you would want to know such information, you can talk at this stage with a clinical geneticist to help you come to a decision. Your treating doctor will refer you. Any finding that concerns a particular hereditary condition should always be confirmed by a laboratory specialising in hereditary conditions. The clinical geneticist will supervise this process. This genetic examination within the study is definitely not a replacement for genetic testing by a clinical geneticist.

5.  What agreements will we make with you and your child?

We want to ensure that the study goes well. That is why we will make the following agreements with you and your child:

- You will contact the study team if your child wants to withdraw from the study.

- You will inform us of any change in address or contact details.

6.  Which side effects, adverse effects or discomforts could your child experience?

When participating in this study, your child will be treated in the standard manner.

Your child may have to undergo extra blood sample collections (for which separate consent must be given). With most children, we use the central line or the PAC for blood sampling. We will also combine blood sample collections for the study with regular blood sample collections where possible.

7.  What are the advantages and disadvantages if your child takes part in the study?

Taking part in the study can have advantages and disadvantages. We will list these below. Think carefully about this and discuss it with others.

Advantages of the study

- Your child’s participation in the study can contribute to increasing our knowledge about soft-tissue tumours in children. The data and results of this study can improve the treatment of children and young adults in the future.

- Because many additional examinations are done and international experts are monitoring the results, we may come to learn more about your child’s disease and refine the diagnosis. In individual cases we may find out something that enables the treating doctor to provide a better therapy for your child.

Disadvantages of the study

- Your child may have to undergo extra blood sample collections. They are not mandatory, and separate consent will be requested for this. With most children, we use the central line or the PAC for blood sampling. We will also combine blood sample collections for the study with regular blood sample collections where possible. Very occasionally, it is not possible to combine the collection with another blood sampling (for example, because your child did not have to be pricked for a long time), so then your child will have to be pricked again for those collections.

8.  When does the study end?

The investigator will let you know if new information about the study becomes available that could be important for you or your child to know. The investigator will then ask you if you want your child to continue taking part. Your child will stop participating in the following situations:

- The entire study has reached an end.

- Your child wants to stop taking part in the study. Or you want your child to stop taking part in the study. This is possible at any time. Please tell the investigator if you decide this. You do not need to say why your child is stopping.

- One of the following authorities decides that the study must stop:

  - the sponsor Princess Máxima Center,

  - the government, or

  - the medical ethics committee that evaluates the study.

The entire study has been completed once all the participants have finished, all tests have been done by the laboratory, and all data collected.

What will happen if your child withdraws from the study?

The investigators will use the data and bodily materials (tumour tissue and blood) that have been collected up to the moment of stopping. If you wish, the bodily materials collected from your child can be destroyed. Please tell the investigator about this.

9.  What will happen after the study?

After participation in the study, your child will be checked according to standard of care. Participation in the study does not influence the treatment and check-ups after the end of the treatment.

Will you receive the results of the study?

If there are results that could lead to a better treatment for your child, we shall inform your child’s treating doctor of this. The doctor is likely to adjust the treatment appropriately and discuss the findings with you.

Once all the data have been processed, the treating doctor will inform you about the most important results of the entire study. Would you prefer not to know them? Please tell your treating doctor about this choice. Then they will not inform you.

10. What will we do with your child’s data and bodily materials?

Is your child taking part in the study? In that case, you also consent to the collection, use and storage of your child’s data and bodily materials.

Which data will we store?

We will store these data:

  - name, gender, address and date of birth

  - data about your child’s health

  - your child’s data (medical and otherwise) that we have collected during the study

Which bodily materials will we store?

We store a piece of your child’s tumour tissue and blood.

Why do we collect, use and store your child’s data and bodily materials?

Your child’s data and bodily materials need to be collected, used and stored in order to answer the questions posed in this study. And in order to publish the results.

How will we protect your child’s privacy?

In order to protect your child’s privacy, the data and bodily materials will be assigned a code. We will use only this code on all your child’s data and bodily materials. The key to the code is stored in a safe spot in the hospital. When we process your child’s data and bodily materials, we will use only this code. The data in reports and publications about the study cannot be traced to your child either.

Who can view your child’s data?

Some people will be able to see your child’s name and other personal data without a code. These are people who check whether the investigators are performing the study properly and in a reliable manner. These individuals will have access to your child’s data:

- Members of the committee that is monitoring the safety of the study.

- An auditor who has been employed by the sponsor.

- National and international regulatory authorities.

They will keep your child’s data confidential. You will be asked to give permission for this access.

How long will your child’s data and bodily materials be stored for?

The data will be stored at the hospital for 25 years after the end of the study. The bodily materials will be stored in the hospital and laboratories involved in the study. They will be stored for a maximum of 25 years to perform new assessments during this study that are related to this study. We will destroy your child’s bodily materials as soon as they are no longer required. Cells and ‘organoids’ cultivated from the collected bodily material samples can continue to be grown, frozen and thawed. These cells can be used for research even after the bodily material has been destroyed. We will use these cells and organoids as long as they grow and are useful for scientific research.

May we use your child’s data and bodily materials for other research?

Your child’s data and any leftover bodily materials can still be useful for other scientific research into the field of soft-tissue tumours after this study has been completed. Therefore, the data and bodily material samples will be stored in the hospital and the involved laboratories for 25 years after the end of the study.

Any further research with data or (leftover) bodily material is always first proposed to a committee. This scientific committee evaluates whether the data and/or bodily material may be used. The committee will evaluate whether the research is designed well and whether the objective fits the MyKids research. We will collaborate only with others after careful consideration and after agreements are defined within a contract.

You can indicate on the informed consent form whether you agree to this. You don't agree with this? Then your child can still take part in this study.

May we approach you and your child for further research after the end of this study?

After the end of this study, we would possibly like to approach you and your child again for further research or a long-term follow-up. You can state on the informed consent form whether you agree to this or not.

Can you withdraw your consent for the use of your child’s data?

You can withdraw your consent for the use of your child’s data at any time, then no more data or bodily material will be collected. This applies to use in this study as well as to their use in future research. Please note: have you withdrawn your consent and have the investigators already collected data for a study? In that case, they may still use those data. The investigators must destroy your child’s bodily materials when you withdraw your consent. Have measurements already been performed using your child’s bodily materials or is the bodily material used for the development of organoids? Then the investigator may continue using those results.

Potentially, we will forward your child’s data to countries outside the European Union

In this study, we may forward your child’s coded data to countries outside the European Union. The privacy laws of the European Union don't apply there. However, your child’s privacy will be protected at an equivalent level. If you do not approve of this, your child cannot participate in the study.

11. Will you or your child receive payment for taking part in the study?

Participation in the study will not cost you anything. Neither you nor your child will receive any compensation if your child takes part in the study. If your child has to come to the hospital specifically for this study or for an extra blood sampling, you will be reimbursed for travel expenses.

12. Is your child insured during the study?

Your child is not insured additionally for this study because participation in the study does not carry any additional risks. That is why the ethics committee does not require the sponsor to arrange additional insurance.

You can first take the time to think about this study. You will then tell the investigator whether you understand the information and whether you want your child to take part or not. Do you want your child to take part? In that case, you will complete the informed consent form that you will find at the end of this information letter.
