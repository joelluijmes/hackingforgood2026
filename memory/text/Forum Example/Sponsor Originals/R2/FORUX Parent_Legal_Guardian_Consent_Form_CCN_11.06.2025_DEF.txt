B.  INFORMATION SECTION.

SUMMARY OF THE TRIAL: KEY INFORMATION

Your child/the minor has been diagnosed with acute Graft-versus-Host Disease (aGvHD), a possible complication that can occur after receiving a stem cell transplant from a donor (called an allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant, or HSCT). Acute GvHD occurs when immune cells from the donor (contained in the graft) attack your child’s/the minor’s body, mistakenly recognizing it as foreign. This immune response causes inflammation and damage to tissues, and it can seriously affect the success of the transplant. Preventive treatments (called prophylaxis) are able to reduce the risk of developing aGvHD, but this complication still affects 30% to 50% of patients receiving a transplant, this variability depending on individual risk factors. aGvHD usually appears within the first 100 days after the transplant (called “classic” aGvHD), but it can also occur later or alongside symptoms of the chronic form (cGvHD), known as "overlap" GvHD. The organs most often affected by aGvHD are the skin, liver, and gastrointestinal (GI) tract. Symptoms can include:

- Skin rashes or redness

- Nausea and vomiting

- Watery (or even bloody) diarrhea

- Yellowing of the skin or eyes (jaundice)

The severity of these symptoms is very important: more severe aGvHD often means a lower chance of recovery and survival.

The standard first treatment for aGvHD is corticosteroids. Unfortunately, these only lead to full recovery in about 40% to 50% of patients. For those patients who don’t respond well to steroids (a condition known as steroid-refractory aGvHD, or SR-aGvHD), the outcome is often poor. If corticosteroids don’t work well enough, doctors may use second-line treatments. These include therapies such as drugs, extracorporeal photopheresis (ECP), mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC), and various other medications that suppress the immune system or target specific biological processes. These treatments can help about half of the patients, but their success rates are still limited.

In addition, even in patients who do improve with steroids or second-line therapies, long-term use of steroids can cause serious side effects such as:

- Increased risk of infections

- High blood sugar (diabetes)

- High blood pressure

- Bone loss (osteoporosis)

- Cataracts

- Muscle weakness

- Slowed growth (in children)

One new medication, Ruxolitinib, has shown better results than other second-line options in clinical studies conducted in patients with SR-aGvHD. Ruxolitinib is a targeted drug taken by mouth that blocks specific proteins (JAK1 and JAK2) involved in the immune response responsible for GvHD development. It is now approved for use in both children and adults who have either acute or chronic GvHD and who haven’t responded well to steroids or other systemic treatments.

Because of its demonstrated effectiveness, the European Society for Blood and Marrow Transplantation (EBMT) now strongly recommends ruxolitinib as the preferred treatment for patients whose aGvHD does not improve with steroids. However, it's important to understand that ruxolitinib does not work for everyone. In a major clinical trial, 38% of patients with SR-GvHD did not show improvement after 28 days, and more than 60% needed additional third-line treatments after 56 days. Also, patients with SR-aGvHD often continue to experience flares (worsening of symptoms) as steroid doses are reduced. This frequently requires continued use of high-dose steroids, which increases the risk of serious infections, cancer recurrence, and other complications.

Because of these challenges, there is an urgent need to find better first-line treatments for children with aGvHD, in order to reduce the cumulative use of corticosteroids and prevent the development of SR-aGvHD.

The FORUX trial (Group R2) is part of a larger international research project (FORUM2) aimed at improving the outcomes of stem cell transplantation in children and young adults with acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL), and it’is specifically focused on pediatric patients (< 18 years) with aGvHD.

- Why am I being asked to authorise the participation in this trial?

We are asking you to give your consent to participate in this clinical trial because we want to verify if the combination of RUXOLITINIB and CORTICOSTEROIDS is safe and more effective when compared to CORTICOSTEROIDS ALONE for the treatment of newly diagnosed aGvHD that has not received any systemic therapy. Your child/the minor is being asked to participate in this trial because s/he shows some clinical characteristics that will be better specified in section C.

The purpose of this study is to find better treatments for aGvHD by addressing the limitations of current first-line therapy with corticosteroids. Specifically, the study aims to reduce the need for long-term steroid use and help prevent the development of steroid-refractory aGvHD (SR-aGvHD).

Ruxolitinib works by targeting several pathways involved in both the innate and adaptive immune systems—two parts of the immune response that play a key role in the development of GvHD. Importantly, preclinical research suggests that ruxolitinib does not interfere with the graft-versus-leukemia (GvL) effect, a beneficial aspect of transplantation where donor immune cells help fight any remaining cancer cells.

This broad immune-modulating activity may explain why ruxolitinib has shown benefits in patients with SR-aGvHD. However, rather than waiting to use it only after steroid resistance develops, we believe that starting ruxolitinib earlier, alongside steroids, could lead to even better outcomes.

- What are the aims of the trial? How many centers and patients will participate?

This trial aims to find out whether starting treatment with both ruxolitinib and corticosteroids right at the beginning of aGvHD can lead to better outcomes than using corticosteroids alone.

The main goals of the study are to:

- Support faster and more effective resolution of aGvHD

- Prevent the disease from progressing to more severe stages

- Enable earlier reduction or discontinuation of immunosuppressive therapy

- Improve overall survival and quality of life

- Promote better recovery of your child’s/the minor’s immune system

The trial is expected to take place in approximately 90 centers across 20 European countries and 192 children and adolescents aged 3 months to 18 years, will be included.

- What is the routine care approach for treating our child’s/the minor’s/the minor’s disease (indicate)?

The standard treatment for aGvHD is based on corticosteroids (such as methylprednisolone or prednisone), which are given by mouth or through a vein. These are usually used alone or together with a class of medicine called calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs), depending on whether your child/the minor is already receiving them and how well they are tolerating them. If your child/the minor had previously stopped or reduced CNIs before developing aGvHD, their doctor may restart it or increase the dose—unless it causes/d significant side effects.

Doctors often try to confirm the diagnosis of aGvHD with a small tissue sample (biopsy) from the affected organ. However, treatment is usually started based on symptoms, even if a biopsy has not yet been done, in order not to delay care.

For moderate to severe aGvHD (Grade II or higher) that affects the gut or liver, the usual first treatment is methylprednisolone at 2 mg per kilogram per day (or a similar dose of prednisone). This dose is typically kept the same for at least 7 days. If your child/the minor improves, the dose is slowly reduced (“tapered”) over several weeks, according to the hospital’s usual practice—often aiming to stop the steroids after 4 to 6 weeks

Taking part in this clinical trial may involve both potential benefits and risks. Below is a summary of the main expected benefits and possible risks associated with participation in this study.

- Expected benefits

By participating in this clinical trial, your child/the minor may receive a combination treatment of ruxolitinib and corticosteroids, which could be more effective than the current standard treatment for acute Graft-versus-Host Disease (aGvHD), which relies on corticosteroids alone.

If effective, this investigational approach may allow to:

- Achieve a faster and more complete resolution of aGvHD

- Obtain earlier recovery of the immune system

- Reduce the need for prolonged use of immunosuppressive medications

Together, these effects may also help preserve the anti-leukemic activity of the donor’s immune cells (also known as the graft-versus-leukemia effect), which plays an important role in preventing cancer relapse after transplant.

Additionally, shortening the duration of corticosteroid use may reduce the risk of serious side effects linked to long-term steroid therapy, such as infections, high blood sugar, bone loss, or growth delays, potentially resulting in a lower risk of complications or death and an improved quality of life for your child.

The FORUX (R2) study, is a Phase III clinical trial, meaning it is designed to rigorously compare the new combination treatment against the current standard of care in a larger group of pediatric patients. Even if your child/the minor does not experience a direct medical benefit, their participation will contribute to advancing scientific knowledge and may help improve treatment options for future children affected by aGvHD.

- Potential risks

We want to make sure that you immediately understand some possible risks: additional information can be found in the next section “What risks can I face if I participate in this trial”?

If you decide to authorize your child/the minor (or the minor under your care) to participate in this trial, it is important to understand that the investigational treatment, namely the combination of ruxolitinib and corticosteroids, may not be more effective than the current standard treatment of corticosteroids alone. - An increased risk of infections.

- A decrease in blood cell counts, such as red cells (anemia), white cells (leukopenia), neutrophils (neutropenia) and platelets (thrombocytopenia).

In previous studies involving pediatric patients with aGvHD, the most common treatment-related side effects included:

- Anemia

- Low white blood cell counts

- Infections

- Fever (pyrexia)

C.  INFORMATION SECTION. FURTHER INFORMATION

1.  What is the purpose of the trial?

The purpose of this clinical trial is to improve treatment for children and adolescents with newly diagnosed acute Graft-versus-Host Disease (aGvHD) by testing whether adding ruxolitinib to standard corticosteroid therapy leads to better outcomes than using corticosteroids alone.

For over 30 years, corticosteroids have been the standard first-line treatment for moderate to severe aGvHD. However, they only work in 30–50% of children, and many patients experience flare-ups when the steroid dose is reduced. Prolonged use of steroids can lead to serious side effects, such as infections, diabetes, high blood pressure, weak bones, slowed growth, and other long-term health problems. The two-year survival rate for children with steroid-refractory aGvHD remains low (20–30%).

Ruxolitinib, an oral medication that blocks specific immune pathways (JAK1 and JAK2), has already shown significant benefits in adults and children with steroid-refractory aGvHD. It works by reducing harmful inflammation and immune system overactivity without interfering with the graft-versus-leukemia (GvL) effect—an important benefit of stem cell transplantation that helps prevent leukemia relapse.

This study aims to find out if starting ruxolitinib early, together with corticosteroids, can:

- Increase the likelihood of treatment success (overall response rate)

- Prevent progression to more severe forms of aGvHD

- Reduce the need for long-term steroid or other immunosuppressive treatments

- Improve survival, immune system recovery, and overall quality of life

By participating in this trial, researchers hope to offer a more effective and safer first-line treatment option for children with aGvHD, and reduce the long-term risks associated with current therapies.

2.  What are the patient groups compared? What is the intervention being tested?

This is a Phase III, randomized, open-label clinical trial. This means that participants will be randomly assigned to one of two treatment groups, with a 50% chance (1 in 2) of receiving the investigational treatment or the standard treatment. This is done to ensure a fair comparison between the two options. This trial is not blinded, meaning that both you and the doctors will know which treatment your child/the minor is receiving. The study aims to enroll a total of 192 patients, with approximately half in each treatment group.

Eligible patients are males or females aged 3 months to under 18 years, transplanted at FORUM2 centers with unmanipulated bone marrow or peripheral blood from a matched donor, following TBI-based or chemotherapy-only conditioning. They must show clinical signs of grade II–IV aGVHD and have confirmed myeloid engraftment.

The two treatment groups are:

- Group 1 – Investigational Treatment:
  Your child/the minor will receive RUXOLITINIB (the investigational drug) in combination with corticosteroids (methylprednisolone 2 mg/kg/day or equivalent prednisone), along with a calcineurin inhibitor (either cyclosporine A or tacrolimus).

  - Ruxolitinib is an oral medication is taken by mouth in tablets or liquid formulation.

  - The exact dosage and schedule of ruxolitinib will be determined based on your child’s/the minor’s age and weight and will be administered daily alongside corticosteroids.

- Group 2 – Standard Treatment (Control Group):
  Your child/the minor will receive corticosteroids alone (methylprednisolone 2 mg/kg/day or equivalent prednisone), along with a calcineurin inhibitor (either cyclosporine A or tacrolimus).

  - This represents the current standard of care used in clinical practice.

The planned treatment period for aGvHD in this study is 24 weeks (about 6 months). Your child’s/the minor’s treatment will begin on Study Day 1 and continue unless one of the following occurs earlier:

- The treatment does not work (no response or worsening of aGvHD)

- There is a mix of improvement and worsening

- There is a flare (return of symptoms)

- Serious side effects occur

- Your child/the minor withdraws from the study or passes away

During the treatment period, your child’s/the minor’s care will be adjusted based on how well they respond to the therapy. The response will first be formally assessed on Day 28 of the study.

If your child/the minor shows improvement (partial or complete response) by Day 28:

- If receiving the investigational treatment (ruxolitinib + corticosteroids): ruxolitinib will continue at the prescribed dose until at least Day 42, while corticosteroids will be tapered gradually after the achievement of a complete response. If your child/the minor remains stable, the doctor may begin gradually reducing (tapering) the dose of ruxolitinib after Day 42, based on their condition and clinical judgment. The taper will ideally be completed by Week 24, unless there's a medical reason to continue for a longer period.

- If receiving corticosteroids alone: Corticosteroids may be tapered gradually following standard guidelines even before Day 28, after achievement of a complete response, depending on your child’s/the minor’s condition.

If your child/the minor does not show improvement by Day 28:

- If in the investigational treatment group (ruxolitinib + corticosteroids): Your child/the minor will be considered as having experienced a treatment failure. In this case, the study doctor may prescribe additional systemic therapies based on your child’s/the minor’s medical condition and clinical needs. Your child/the minor will then enter the long-term follow-up phase and will continue to be closely monitored as part of the clinical trial.

- If in the corticosteroids-only group: The doctor may start ruxolitinib at any time during the treatment period if your child/the minor meets the criteria for steroid-refractory aGvHD (when steroids are not or no longer effective). If ruxolitinib is started before Day 28, this will be recorded as a treatment failure (event) for analysis purposes. If started after Day 28, it will be considered a formal "cross-over" to the investigational treatment.

Children who cross over to ruxolitinib after starting in the corticosteroids-only group will follow the same treatment and tapering plan as those who started with ruxolitinib. They will be monitored weekly for 8 weeks after starting ruxolitinib, and then monthly until tapering is complete.

In all cases, patients receiving ruxolitinib will continue taking corticosteroids and a calcineurin inhibitor (either cyclosporine A or tacrolimus) as per standard practice, unless your physician identifies specific contraindications. Tapering of these drugs will be done based on how well your child/the minor is responding to treatment.

All biological samples collected from your child/the minor will be coded and pseudoanonymized immediately after collection. This means their name and personal identifiers will be replaced by a unique code, known only to the study doctor. This coding system helps protect your child/the minor’s privacy, while allowing important results to be shared with you if needed.

Some samples will be analyzed at the hospital where your child/the minor is being treated, while others may be sent to the coordinating study center located at Bambino Gesù Children’s Hospital.

The storage and handling of your child/the minor’s data and biological samples will be done in full compliance with applicable data protection laws, including the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the European Union (see also question 10). 4.  What risks can I our child/the minor face if I/we authorise participation in the trial?

Participation in this clinical trial may involve risks related to the study drug (ruxolitinib), other medications used during treatment, and procedures such as blood sampling. Side effects may vary from person to person. This document includes both common side effects and rare but serious side effects known to be associated with ruxolitinib. You are encouraged to discuss any concerns with the study doctor, including possible side effects not listed here.

Many side effects resolve shortly after stopping or adjusting the medication. However, some side effects may be serious, long-lasting, or permanent, and in rare cases, could be life-threatening.

Side effects of ruxolitinib

In patients treated for other conditions (such as myelofibrosis or polycythemia vera), very common side effects (occurring in more than 1 in 10 people) included:

- Low red blood cell count (anemia), which may cause fatigue, pale skin, or shortness of breath

- Low white blood cell count (neutropenia), which may increase the risk of infection

- Low platelet count (thrombocytopenia), which may lead to easy bruising or bleeding

- Urinary tract infections

- Increased cholesterol or triglyceride levels

- Headache, dizziness, weight gain

- Constipation, high blood pressure, altered liver test results

- Shingles (herpes zoster), which may cause painful rash or blisters

In most cases, changes in blood cell counts are reversible and can be managed by temporarily reducing or stopping ruxolitinib. Your child/the minor will be monitored regularly through blood tests, and if their cell counts do not recover quickly, ruxolitinib may be paused longer or restarted at a lower dose.

Common side effects (occurring in 1 to 10 in 100 patients) included:

- Excessive intestinal gas (flatulence)

- Reduction in all three types of blood cells (pancytopenia)

In rare cases (less than 1 in 100), tuberculosis has been reported. Symptoms may include chronic cough, coughing up blood, fever, night sweats, and weight loss. Inform the study doctor immediately if your child/the minor shows any of these symptoms.

Suddenly stopping ruxolitinib may also lead to health risks. In patients with aGvHD or myelofibrosis, abrupt discontinuation has been associated with a return of symptoms or inflammatory reactions, including anxiety, insomnia, weakness, or worsening disease. If treatment needs to be stopped, it will be done under close medical supervision.

The effect of ruxolitinib on viral replication in people with chronic hepatitis B is not known. If your child/the minor has ever had viral hepatitis, this must be discussed with the study doctor. Children with active viral infection will not be treated with ruxolitinib.

There have been rare reports of progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML), a serious brain infection, among patients undergoing allogeneic stem cell transplantation, including some receiving ruxolitinib. While it is not clear if ruxolitinib caused PML, symptoms such as confusion, speech difficulty, weakness on one side of the body, or vision problems must be reported immediately.

Cases of non-melanoma skin cancers (NMSC), including basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and the rare Merkel cell carcinoma, have been reported in patients treated with ruxolitinib. These may also be related to prior treatments or the underlying disease. Inform the doctor if you notice any new or unusual skin lesions.

In animal studies, bone growth delay and fractures were observed in very young animals treated with high doses of ruxolitinib. Although these findings occurred at higher doses than those used in this study, the greatest concern applies to children under 2 years of age, especially those under 1 year. Bone growth will be monitored, and potential effects are expected to be reversible, although full catch-up growth may not always occur.

Clinical experience in pediatric patients with aGvHD

In a recent clinical trial of ruxolitinib in children with treatment-naïve or steroid-refractory aGvHD:

- All patients experienced at least one adverse event (AE)

- 51.1% had treatment-related AEs, most commonly anemia (20%), low neutrophil count (17.8%), and low white blood cell count (15.6%)

- 86.7% experienced a severe AE (grade ≥3), with 40% of those considered treatment-related

- Serious AEs occurred in 53.3% of patients, including fever, kidney injury, sepsis, and viral cystitis

- Infections were reported in 71.1% of patients, with 14 having grade ≥3 infections

Despite these risks, many patients were able to reduce or completely stop corticosteroids during treatment. By day 56, 75.6% had reduced steroid use by at least 50%, and 37.8% were off steroids entirely. By the end of the treatment period, 62.2% had fully discontinued steroids.

These results suggest that ruxolitinib offers clinically meaningful benefits with a manageable safety profile, although vigilant monitoring for side effects—especially infections—is essential.

As part of the study, your child/the minor will undergo approximately 16 blood draws. The volume of blood collected will depend on their age and the specific visit. Risks include fainting, discomfort, bruising, and rarely, infection or blood clots at the site of collection. Blood pressure cuffs may cause temporary discomfort or bruising.

In rare cases, a healthcare worker may be accidentally exposed to your child’s/the minor’s blood. If this occurs, the hospital will follow its standard safety procedures. You will be informed and advised of any actions necessary to ensure your child’s/the minor’s and the staff’s safety. Confidentiality will be maintained.

Pregnancy and reproductive safety

Pregnant or breastfeeding girls may not participate in this study. If your daughter is of childbearing potential, she must confirm she is not pregnant and must agree to avoid pregnancy during the study.

Animal studies suggest that ruxolitinib could harm a fetus or newborn. If your daughter becomes pregnant or suspects she may be pregnant during the study or within 30 days of completing treatment, she must immediately inform the study doctor and stop taking the study drug. Continued treatment during pregnancy is not allowed.

The doctor will monitor her and her baby’s health throughout the pregnancy. You and your daughter will be asked to sign a separate pregnancy follow-up consent to allow the doctor to collect relevant information about the outcome of the pregnancy and the baby’s health, if appropriate.

There is currently no evidence that ruxolitinib is present in human sperm.

During treatment with ruxolitinib, you must immediately inform the study doctor if your child/the minor:

- Develops unusual bruising, bleeding, extreme fatigue, shortness of breath, or frequent infections

- Shows signs of shingles (painful skin rash with blisters)

- Has a persistent cough with blood, fever, night sweats, or weight loss (possible signs of tuberculosis)

- Experiences confusion, weakness, visual disturbances, or trouble speaking or walking (possible signs of PML)

- Has any changes in prescribed medications or develops any new medical issues

- Develops new or changing skin lesions, which may require further examination

- Becomes pregnant or, if male, is known or suspected to have fathered a pregnancy during the study
