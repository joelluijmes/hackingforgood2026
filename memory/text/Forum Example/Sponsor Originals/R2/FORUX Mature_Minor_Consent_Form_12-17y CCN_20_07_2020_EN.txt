A.  QUESTIONS AND ANSWERS: KEY INFORMATION

Dear Young Person
You’ve been diagnosed with acute Graft-versus-Host Disease, or aGvHD. This is a complication that can happen after a stem cell transplant from a donor (called an allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant, or HSCT). When you have aGvHD, some of the donor’s immune cells (which are part of the transplant) attack your body because they see it as “foreign.” This can cause inflammation and damage in different parts of your body, and it can make your recovery more difficult.

Doctors try to prevent aGvHD by giving medicines (called prophylaxis) after the transplant. But even with these preventive treatments, about 30% to 50% of people who get a transplant still develop aGvHD. It usually happens within the first 100 days after the transplant (this is called “classic” aGvHD), but it can also show up later or along with a longer-lasting form called chronic GvHD (or cGvHD).

The parts of the body most often affected by aGvHD are:

- Your skin

- Your liver

- Your gut (digestive system)

You might have symptoms like:

- Skin rashes or redness

- Nausea or throwing up

- Watery or bloody diarrhea

- Yellowing of your skin or eyes (this is called jaundice)

How serious these symptoms are can affect how well you recover from the transplant. More severe symptoms usually mean a harder recovery.

The usual first treatment for aGvHD is a type of medicine called corticosteroids (like prednisone). These work well for some people—but not for everyone. In fact, only about 40% to 50% of people fully get better with steroids. If the steroids don’t work, the condition is called steroid-refractory aGvHD (SR-aGvHD), and it's harder to treat.

If that happens, doctors try second-line treatments, like:

- Special immune-suppressing drugs

- Extracorporeal photopheresis (ECP), a type of light-based blood treatment

- Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC), which help reduce inflammation

- Other targeted therapies

These treatments help about half the patients, but not all. Also, using steroids for a long time can cause serious side effects, like:

- Infections

- High blood sugar (diabetes)

- High blood pressure

- Weak bones (osteoporosis)

- Cataracts

- Muscle weakness

- Slower growth (especially in kids and teens)

A newer drug called ruxolitinib has shown better results for people with SR-aGvHD. It’s a pill that blocks certain proteins (called JAK1 and JAK2) involved in the immune system response that causes GvHD. It’s now approved for children and adults with either acute or chronic GvHD when other treatments haven’t worked.

Because of this, experts in Europe (the EBMT) recommend ruxolitinib as the preferred option when steroids don’t help. But even this drug doesn’t work for everyone. In one major study:

- 38% of patients didn’t get better after 28 days

- Over 60% still needed more treatments after 56 days

Also, some patients still have flare-ups of aGvHD when doctors try to lower the steroid dose. This often means they have to stay on high-dose steroids longer, which increases the risk of other serious problems like infections or relapse of the original disease.

That’s why researchers are trying to find better first-line treatments for kids and teens with aGvHD. The goal is to reduce the need for steroids and to prevent cases from becoming steroid-refractory.

This brings us to the FORUX trial (Group R2). It’s part of a larger international research study called FORUM2, which is looking for better treatments for children and young adults with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) who have had a stem cell transplant. The FORUX trial (Group R2) is just for patients 18 or younger who develop aGvHD after transplant.

- What is the aim of this clinical trial?

  We’re inviting you to take part in this clinical trial because we want to see if using two medicines together, ruxolitinib and corticosteroids, works better and is just as safe as using corticosteroids alone to treat aGvHD when it first appears.

  Right now, the first treatment for aGvHD is usually corticosteroids. But these don’t work well for everyone, and they can cause serious side effects if used for a long time. That’s why researchers are looking for better ways to treat aGvHD from the start—before it becomes harder to treat.

  Ruxolitinib is a medicine that helps calm down the immune system by blocking certain signals (called pathways) that are involved in causing GvHD. These signals normally help your body fight infections, but in GvHD, they’re too active and attack your own tissues.

  An important thing about ruxolitinib is that it does not seem to interfere with the “graft-versus-leukemia” (GvL) effect. That’s the part of the transplant where the donor’s immune cells can still help fight any leftover cancer cells in your body. That’s a good thing.

  We already know ruxolitinib can help some people who don’t get better with steroids. But in this trial, we want to test whether starting ruxolitinib right away, together with steroids, might give even better results—before steroid resistance has a chance to develop.

- What are the objectives of the trial? Who else will participate? How long will it last?

  This study is trying to answer this big question:

  Is it better to treat aGvHD right away with both ruxolitinib and corticosteroids, instead of corticosteroids alone?

  The main goals of the study are to:

- Help aGvHD symptoms go away faster and more effectively

- Stop the disease from getting worse

- Make it possible to reduce or stop immune-suppressing medications earlier

- Improve survival and overall quality of life

- Support better and faster recovery of your immune system

- What happens if I decide to participate in the trial? How long will it last?

If you decide to take part in this study, here’s what you need to know.

This is a clinical trial testing two different treatments for aGvHD (a complication after a stem cell transplant). The researchers want to find out if adding a medicine called ruxolitinib to the usual treatment (called corticosteroids) works better than using corticosteroids alone.

You’ll be randomly placed in one of two treatment groups—kind of like flipping a coin. You have a 50% chance of being in either group. You and your doctors will know which treatment you’re getting.

The two treatment groups are:

Group 1 – New combination treatment:

- You’ll get ruxolitinib (a pill or liquid you take by mouth), plus corticosteroids, plus another medicine that helps control the immune system (called cyclosporine A or tacrolimus).

Group 2 – Standard treatment:

- You’ll get only corticosteroids, plus cyclosporine A or tacrolimus. This is the usual way doctors treat aGvHD right now.

The dose of ruxolitinib will be based on your age and weight.

Before starting the treatment

Before starting the trial, you’ll go through a screening period that lasts up to 7 days. During this time, the study team will check if you’re eligible to join by doing:

- A review of your medical history

- A physical exam

- Blood tests

- Other checks to confirm aGvHD

Sometimes, a biopsy of the affected area (like the skin or gut) may be needed, but this is only done if necessary. Biopsy is usually performed under general anesthesia. You can still join the study without a biopsy if your doctor agrees.

No study-related procedures will happen unless you and your parents/guardians give written consent.

How long does the study last?

The main treatment period lasts about 6 months (24 weeks). You’ll need to come to the hospital or clinic up to 13 times:

- Once a week for the first 8 weeks

- Once every 4 weeks after that until Week 24

Your doctor will check how well you’re doing, especially on Day 28.

If you're improving:

- Treatment continues and doses may be slowly reduced (tapered).

- If you're on ruxolitinib, it will be continued at least until Day 42, and possibly up to Week 24 or longer if needed.

If you're not improving:

- Your treatment might change.

- If you started with ruxolitinib and steroids, your doctor might add other treatments.

- If you were on steroids alone, your doctor may decide to start ruxolitinib (this is called “crossing over”). From that point, you’ll follow the same plan as patients who started with ruxolitinib.

You’ll keep taking the immune-suppressing medicine (cyclosporine A or tacrolimus) unless your doctor says otherwise.

After the treatment period

About 1 month after your last dose, your doctor will check in with you—by phone or at a visit—for a safety check.

Then, you’ll be asked to return for long-term follow-up visits at:

- 9 months, 12 months, 18 months, and 24 months after treatment starts.

These visits help the doctors keep track of your health and recovery.

Some visits may take 2 to 3 hours. Your first treatment visit may be longer (up to 9–10 hours) because of extra testing and observation.

What else will happen during the study?

In addition to regular blood tests, some extra samples will be taken for research, to help doctors learn more about:

- How your immune system recovers

- How your body processes ruxolitinib (pharmacokinetics, or PK)

- Certain biomarkers (clues in your blood that help doctors understand diseases)

When PK samples are needed, you’ll take your ruxolitinib dose at the hospital, not at home. If you ever miss a dose or take it at the wrong time, tell your doctor right away.

All samples will be coded to protect your identity. Your name will not appear on them—only a special code known by your doctor.

Some samples will be tested at your hospital, others may be sent to Bambino Gesù Children’s Hospital, the study’s main center.

What happens to leftover blood samples?

Some blood samples may be stored and used later for research related to this study, for up to 15 years. They might help:

- Improve how we diagnose or treat aGvHD

- Compare results with other studies

No genetic testing will be done unless you give extra written permission later.

You can choose to:

- Or ask that they be destroyed or fully anonymized (so no one can link them back to you)

You can withdraw from the study at any time. This won’t affect your regular care in any way.

If new results come out that could affect your future health or medical choices, the study team will inform you.

- Are there any rules to respect?

Yes. If you join the study, there are some important rules to follow to keep you safe and to make sure the study results are reliable.

Here’s what you need to do:

- Go to all your scheduled study visits. These visits are important for checking how the treatment is working and how you’re feeling.

- Take your medicine exactly as the doctor tells you. Whether it’s a tablet or a liquid, don’t skip any doses. If you miss a dose by accident or feel unwell after taking it, tell your study doctor right away.

- Tell the study team about any other medicines or supplements you're using. This includes:

  - Prescription drugs

  - Over-the-counter medicines (like painkillers or cold medicine)

  - Herbal or natural products

  - Vitamins

Also, tell them if you stop taking any regular medication during the study.

- Let the doctor know right away if you feel anything unusual or different, even if it doesn’t seem serious. This could be a side effect or something important for your care.

If you're old enough to have children:

- Girls: You must not get pregnant or breastfeed during the study. The doctor will talk to you about using the right birth control methods to stay safe.

- Boys: You must not try to father a child during the study. The doctor will explain what precautions to take.

By agreeing to be in the study, you’re saying that you will:

- Follow the doctor’s instructions

- Come to your study visits

- Stick to all the study rules

These steps are really important for your safety and for the success of the research

- What benefits can I expect?

If you take part in this study, you may receive a new combination treatment that includes ruxolitinib and corticosteroids. This could work better than the current standard treatment, which uses corticosteroids alone.

If the combination works well, it might:

- Help your aGvHD symptoms get better more quickly and completely

- Help your immune system recover sooner

- Reduce the need for long-term use of medicines that weaken your immune system

All of these things could help you:

- Avoid serious side effects from steroids, like infections, high blood sugar, weak bones, or slowed growth

- Lower the chance of relapse (when leukemia comes back)

- Improve your overall quality of life during and after treatment

This study is a Phase III trial, which means it is carefully designed to compare two treatments in a large group of children and teens. If the new combination works better, it could become the new standard way to treat aGvHD in kids.

Even if you don’t personally benefit from the treatment, your participation will still help researchers learn more about how to treat aGvHD, which could help other children like you in the future.

- Will I take risks if I participate in the trial?

In a study like this, every adolescent can react differently after taking the investigational drug and could have side effects or ailments, even serious ones, that the trial doctor cannot predict at the moment. For this reason you will be carefully monitored. Many of these reactions disappear immediately after stopping the therapies. In some cases, adverse reactions may instead become more serious, long-lasting, or never go away; they may require hospitalization and even (rarely) be fatal. For this reason, if side effects occur during the study, you must immediately report them to your parents and to the investigating doctor, so they may decide to suspend the administration of the drug and give you any necessary treatment. In addition, you and your parents will be notified promptly if information becomes available during the trial that could influence the decision to participate in the study.

What are the possible side effects of ruxolitinib?

Most side effects go away after stopping or adjusting the medication. But some can be long-lasting, serious, or very rarely, life-threatening.

Very common side effects (happen in more than 1 in 10 people):

- Low red blood cells (anemia): may cause tiredness, pale skin, or shortness of breath

- Low white blood cells: can raise the risk of infections

- Low platelets: may cause bruising or bleeding easily

- Urinary tract infections (UTIs)

- Increased cholesterol or fat in the blood

- Headaches, dizziness, weight gain

- Constipation, high blood pressure, or abnormal liver test results

- Shingles (painful rash or blisters)

Doctors will check your blood regularly. If blood counts drop too low, your doctor may pause or adjust the medication.

Less common side effects (1 to 10 out of 100 people):

- Too much gas (flatulence)

- Low levels of all types of blood cells (called pancytopenia)

Rare side effects (less than 1 in 100 people):

- Tuberculosis (TB): cough, fever, night sweats, or weight loss

- Severe brain infection (PML): symptoms like confusion, speech problems, vision changes, or weakness

- Skin cancers: new or changing moles or bumps—tell your doctor if you notice anything strange on your skin

Other possible risks:

- Stopping ruxolitinib suddenly could cause symptoms like weakness, anxiety, or return of disease, always stop under a doctor’s supervision

- It may not be safe if you’ve had hepatitis B or other viral infections—make sure to tell your doctor if this applies to you

- In animal studies, ruxolitinib slowed down bone growth. This may affect very young children, but bone growth will be monitored in everyone

What have we seen in kids who took ruxolitinib in past studies?

In a recent study with children who had aGvHD:

- All had at least one side effect

- About half had side effects related to ruxolitinib (most often low blood counts)

- Over half had serious side effects (like fever, infections, or kidney issues)

- Infections were common, about 7 in 10 kids got one, but many kids were able to reduce or stop taking steroids thanks to ruxolitinib

Other risks in the study

You’ll have about 16 blood draws during the study. This might cause:

- Discomfort, bruising, or dizziness

- Very rarely, infection or blood clots at the needle site

If any hospital staff are accidentally exposed to your blood (this is very rare), they’ll follow safety rules to protect everyone. Your identity will stay confidential.

What if I’m able to get pregnant or make someone pregnant?

- Girls: You can’t be pregnant or breastfeeding in this study. You’ll need to use birth control during the study. If you think you might be pregnant, tell the study doctor right away—you’ll need to stop the medication, and your health (and the baby’s) will be followed closely.

- Boys: You should avoid getting someone pregnant during the study. There's no current evidence that ruxolitinib affects sperm, but it’s still important to take precautions.

If a pregnancy happens during the study, extra consent will be needed to collect follow-up health information about the baby, if appropriate

If you decide not to take part in this trial:

- You will still be treated at your usual hospital

- You will still receive the best approved treatment available for aGvHD

- You may still be able to join other parts of the FORUM2 study, or other clinical trials, as long as you meet the criteria

If you do join this trial and then decide to stop, the doctors will keep taking care of you and will continue to offer the best treatment options based on your situation.
