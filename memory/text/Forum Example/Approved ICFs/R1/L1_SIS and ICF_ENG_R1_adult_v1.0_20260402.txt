CHAPTER I – DESCRIPTION OF THE TRIAL AND YOUR RIGHTS WHEN PARTICIPATING

Why is this sub-study being conducted?

This sub-study is part of the larger FORUM2 platform trial, which aims to optimize the way stem cell transplantation is performed for children and young people with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL).

For patients with a diagnosis of high-risk or relapsed ALL, an allogeneic stem cell transplant (HSCT) is one of the recommended treatments. This means that you will receive healthy stem cells from a suitable donor. This approach is part of routine care for this kind of ALL and has been shown to improve long-term survival.

Before the transplant, a so-called conditioning treatment has been given. This prepares the body by eliminating remaining leukaemia cells and suppressing the immune system, to allow donor cells to grow.

Conditioning usually involves a combination of total body irradiation (TBI) and chemotherapy. The current standard approach is 12 Gray (Gy) irradiation in combination with a chemotherapy called etoposide (12 Gy TBI plus etoposide), which has shown to have very good results in preventing relapse. However, radiation is also associated with an increased risk of acute side effects (organ damage, nausea, mucositis, skin toxicity, and infections) and long-term side effects such as growth disorders, hormonal imbalances, infertility and late organ damage

This sub-study of the FORUM2 platform trial is being conducted to find out whether a lower radiation dose of 8 Gy (combined with the drug Etoposide, also called VP 16) is equally effective in preventing relapse while reducing long-term side effects. Several smaller trials have shown promising results of reducing the radiation dose while maintaining the efficacy.

Group R1 focuses specifically on the question: Is 8 Gy TBI as effective as 12 Gy TBI in preventing leukaemia relapse, but with fewer harmful effects?

This substudy is expected to enroll around 1,000 children and adolescents aged 2 to 25 years, in multiple centres across Europe and beyond. All participating centres are experienced transplant centres following strict international protocols.

The main goal (primary objective) is to compare survival without relapse or secondary cancers between the two groups after four years.

Secondary objectives include comparing side effects, infections, transplant-related complications, organ function, and quality of life.

Why am I being asked to take part?

You have been diagnosed with acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL). Although treatment so far has led to complete remission (meaning that no leukaemia cells are currently detectable), there remains a significant risk that the leukaemia could come back based on the characteristics of your disease. To give you the best chance of a long-term cure, an allogeneic stem cell transplant (HSCT) is recommended, in which you will receive healthy stem cells from a suitable donor.

To prepare for the HSCT, a standard total body irradiation with a dose of 12 Gy (GY is the unit of irradiation) is given in addition to standard chemotherapy (Etoposide, also called VP 16).

This sub-study involves a randomised comparison: if you agree to your participation, you will be assigned at random to receive either 8 Gy or 12 Gy of radiation before the transplant. All participants receive the same chemotherapy drug, etoposide. This kind of trial design — called a controlled, randomized trial — is the most reliable way to compare medical treatments.

The investigator or trial staff will discuss with you the requirements to be allowed to enter the trial.

Do I have to take part in a trial?

Your participation in a trial is voluntary and must remain free of any coercion. This means that you have the right not to take part in the trial or to withdraw at any time without giving a reason, even if you previously agreed to take part. Your decision will not affect your relationship with the investigator or your treating physician nor will it affect the quality of your future medical care.

If other treatments are available for HSCT for ALL, the investigator or his/her delegate will discuss these treatments with you.

If you agree to participate, your investigator will inform your treating physicians. You then cannot participate in another interventional clinical trial without informing the investigator or trial staff. You also need to communicate all relevant information related to the state of your health, other medication taken or the symptoms experienced.

What will happen during the sub-study? 

If you choose to participate in this sub-study, the only difference compared to standard care will be the dose of total body irradiation (TBI) used as part of the conditioning treatment before the stem cell transplantation. You will receive either the standard dose (12 Gy) or the reduced dose (8 Gy) of TBI, depending on the random assignment.

All other aspects of the transplantation procedure — including the chemotherapy drug (etoposide), the transplantation itself, supportive care during the hospital stay, and follow-up after discharge — will be identical to the treatment you would receive outside the trial.

Importantly, participation in this sub-study will not result in any additional examinations, procedures, or follow-up visits beyond what is already required for a standard of care stem cell transplantation. This sub-study does not involve any experimental drugs, additional invasive procedures or extra appointments. You will be monitored in the same way and with the same frequency as any other patient undergoing a stem cell transplantation outside this trial.

Participants are randomly assigned to one of two treatment groups. Both groups will receive a standard conditioning treatment before the transplantation, which includes a chemotherapy drug called etoposide. This drug will be given intravenously at a dose and schedule in line with current standard of care transplantation protocols.

The difference between the two groups is the dose of total body irradiation (TBI) applied along with etoposide:

- One group will receive the standard dose of 12 Gray (Gy) TBI, which is the current standard treatment used in clinical practice for patients undergoing HSCT.

- The other group will receive a reduced dose of 8 Gy, which is the investigational treatment being tested in this trial.

TBI is given through external radiation in multiple fractions over 2 to 3 days, and is administered by radiation oncology specialists before the transplantation. The aim is to compare how well the two different TBI doses work in preventing relapse and to examine whether the lower dose can reduce acute and long-term side effects without compromising effectiveness.

This is a randomized controlled sub-study. Participants will be randomly assigned to receive either the standard 12 Gy TBI or the investigational 8 Gy TBI, each with equal probability (1:1 randomization). The probability is 50:50. The assignment is not influenced by the patient, their family, or the treating physician. The sub-study is not blinded, meaning both patients and the medical team will know which dose has been assigned.

Both TBI doses are administered as part of a defined standard of care transplantation protocol and followed by the stem cell infusion. After the transplantation, all patients will receive the same supportive care and monitoring.

Approximately 1,000 patients are expected to participate in this sub-study, including about 4-5 participants each year in Belgium, with roughly 500 patients in each treatment group. Clinical data will be collected on how you feel and which side effects you experience until 5 years after transplantation.

If you meet all the conditions required to be enrolled in this sub-study and agree to take part, you will undergo the above-mentioned treatment. There are no trial specific procedures or visits. Only the dose of TBI is different between the 2 groups (8 or 12 Gy). If you will not participate in this sub-study, you will receive the standard of care dose of TBI, 12Gy.

Standard of care procedures or examinations for your condition (i.e. standard of care) will be charged to you or reimbursed by your mutual insurance fund (Belgian social security). If you need more details or if you are not affiliated with a mutual insurance fund (Belgian social security), please contact the trial staff.

Will I benefit from the sub-study? 

The information obtained during a trial may contribute to improve care and outcomes of yourself and future patients with ALL undergoing a stem cell transplantation. Possible benefits of participating include the chance that you will receive a lower radiation dose that may reduce the risk of long-term complications — assuming the 8 Gy dose proves to be as effective. However, this cannot be guaranteed, and there may be no direct benefit for you.

What are the possible risks and discomforts of taking part?

1.  What are the possible side effects of Total Body Irradiation

Participation in a trial involves some risk. Participation in this sub-study involves undergoing an allogeneic stem cell transplant (HSCT), preceded by conditioning treatment with chemotherapy and total body irradiation (TBI). These treatments are known to be highly intensive and associated with a range of short- and long-term risks. While all treatments used in the sub-study are based on current clinical standard of care, the investigational aspect of this sub-study is the comparison between two different TBI doses: 12 Gy (standard) and 8 Gy (reduced). Below is a detailed description of the foreseeable risks associated with participation.

TBI is used to prepare the body to accept donor stem cells by suppressing the immune system and eliminating remaining leukemia cells. Radiation affects not only cancer cells but also healthy tissues, which may lead to acute and delayed complications.

- Short-term (acute) side effects: Fatigue, nausea, vomiting, decreased appetite, irritation of the mucous membranes, and skin redness or dryness.

- Long-term side effects: These may develop months to years after treatment and include:

  - Endocrine disorders such as hypothyroidism or growth hormone deficiency.

  - Delayed or impaired growth and physical development, particularly in younger children.

  - Infertility or reduced fertility due to damage to the reproductive organs.

  - Cataracts (clouding of the lens in the eye) that may require surgery.

  - Lung or kidney dysfunction, usually mild but sometimes persistent.

  - Secondary malignancies (new cancers) later in life — these are rare but serious.

The risk and severity of acute and long-term effects are generally higher with 12 Gy TBI compared to 8 Gy. The goal of the sub-study is to determine whether the lower dose is equally effective while causing fewer of these complications. However, since conditioning with 8 Gy has not been tested in randomized controlled trial yet, the lower dose of irradiation might be associated with an increased risk of relapse or and increased risk of graft versus host disease (GVHD). This sub-study is closely monitored and in case the risk of relapse or GVHD is too high, the study will be stopped.

Therefore, it is very important that you report any new or worsened health problems immediately to the investigator, regardless of whether or not you think it has to do with the sub-study, and even when it is already described in this document. If you need to use other medication, discuss this with the investigator beforehand. If, for any reason, you consult another treating physician during the trial you must inform him/her that you are taking part in a trial. This could be important in determining a diagnosis and giving you the correct treatment if needed.

1.  What are the possible risks or discomforts of the examinations during the trial?

There are no trial specific examinations during the sub-study. Only examinations within the standard of care will be performed. There will be no additional risks or discomforts compared to the standard of care.

2.  Can I take other medicines during the trial?

Do not hesitate to ask your investigator for more explanation about the use of medicines or and food supplements (including aromatherapy, phytotherapy, homeopathy..).

We want to ensure that the trial runs smoothly. That is why we will make the following agreements with you:

• During this trial, you won’t participate in another medical scientific trial,

4.  Can I get pregnant, conceive a child, or can I breastfeed during the trial?

The conditioning regimen, particularly TBI, can damage reproductive organs and can cause significant harm to an unborn child. If you don’t participate in this sub-study, you will receive TBI outside this study and it is also important not to become pregnant or not to cause a pregnancy outside this study.

Loss of fertility is expected in both males and females after HSCT, though individual risk depends on age, dose, and sex. In a separate appointment, your treating physician will discuss in detail possible interventions for fertility preservation.

If you are a women, you must not become pregnant during the trial and for at least 12 months after completing treatment. In addition, you must not have egg cells stored for your own use or donation after the start of the treatment and for at least 12 months after the last treatment. You must not breastfeed during treatment.

If you are a men, you must not cause a pregnancy or donate sperm during the trial and for at least 6 months after completing treatment.

If you can become pregnant and are heterosexually active, or if you have a partner who can become pregnant, then the treating physician or nurse will explain the different types of contraceptives that you should use during and after the trial.

Your investigator will discuss with you the suitable forms of contraception. If you decide that you will participate in this trial, you or your female partner of the participant that can become pregnant, must use one of the recognised contraceptive methods (to prevent you or the female partner from becoming pregnant). If you/your partner is using oral hormonal contraception, you/your partner will need to use a barrier method such as condoms too.

If you or your partner becomes pregnant during this trial, you must tell the investigator or the trial staff immediately. If you become pregnant, the treating physician will discuss what next steps to take with the treatment. The investigator will monitor the pregnancy and inform the sponsor about its outcome. For safety reasons, the investigator wants to monitor the pregnancy and its outcome and may be required to share this data with the sponsor to comply with regulations on monitoring and reporting safety information. You/your partner will receive a separate information letter regarding this, and a separate consent will have to be granted.

What If something goes wrong within the trial?

Even if there is no fault, the sponsor is liable for harm caused to you whether directly or indirectly related to your participation in the trial. The sponsor has taken an appropriate insurance (a so called “No Fault insurance”) for this liability. A copy of the insurance certificate can be obtained from the investigator or trial staff.

If you (or in the event of death, your rightful claimants) seek compensation for a harm to your health as a direct or indirect result of participating in the trial, you must inform your investigator or trial staff promptly.

If the investigator believes that a link between the new or worsened health problem(s) and the trial is possible, he/she will inform the trial sponsor. The sponsor will then immediately initiate the declaration procedure to its insurance company. If the company considers it necessary, it will appoint an expert to assess whether there is a link between your reported health problem(s) and the trial. The insurance does not cover the natural progression of your disease/condition or the known side effects of the treatment you would have received without taking part to the trial (that is your standard treatment).

Whenever you feel it is appropriate or if you or your rightful claimants disagree either with the investigator or with the expert appointed by the insurance company, you may contact the insurance company or proceedings may be brought against the insurance company. You will find the contact details on the front page of this form.

What if other treatment options or new information become available during the course of the trial?

During the course of the trial, important new information might become available, possibly affecting your decision to (further) participate. For example, other treatments or important new information may become available. It is the duty of the investigator to discuss this new information with you and to give you the opportunity to re-consider your participation in the trial.

If you decide to stop taking part in the trial or if you are no longer able to participate, your investigator will see to it that you continue to receive the best possible medical care.

Can my participation in the trial end prematurely?

As explained in detail below, your trial participation may end prematurely when

- you decide to withdraw your consent or,

- the investigator decides to end your trial participation, or

- other entities interrupt or end the trial.

In any case, if your trial participation ends prematurely, the investigator will discuss your future medical care with you. Data collected before you terminate your participation will continue to be used as described in this form. This is to avoid skewing / biasing results of the trial (as described in chapter I. § 12.4.).

Depending on your situation, the investigator will discuss with you whether follow-up visits or procedures are needed.

1.  You decide to withdraw your consent

You are entitled to withdraw your consent for any reason, at any time, without having to justify your decision. However, for your safety, you should inform the investigator of your decision. Although it is not mandatory, it may be useful for the investigator and for the sponsor to know the reason of this decision (for example side effects, frequency of clinical visits,…).

If you withdraw your consent, please discuss with the investigator to evaluate the practical modalities of the withdrawal (in light of your situation). No new data will be collected and sent to the sponsor.

2.  The investigator decides to end your trial participation

The investigator may end your trial participation because

- he/she thinks it is better for you

- he/she determines that you are not following the instructions given to participants, or

- any other reason that will be explained,

- the entire study should be discontinued for all participants (see next paragraph).

If that happens, the trial staff will explain why and ensure appropriate follow-up.

1.  Other entities may interrupt or end the trial

The sponsor and the competent Belgian health authorities may interrupt or end the trial because

- the information gathered shows that the sub-study is not effective (does not deliver a sufficient level of improvement in the health of the trial participants),

- there are more (serious) side effects than anticipated, or

- any other reason that will be duly motivated by such party.

Which treatment will I get after my participation in the trial? 

If you stop the study, the investigator will assess your health. If necessary, he/she will prescribe you the best standard treatment available.

Will my participation in the trial involve extra costs for me?

As described in section 4, all visits, examinations and the treatment are standard of care. Only the dose of TBI is different (12Gy versus 8 Gy). The standard procedures or examinations for your condition (i.e. standard of care) will be charged to you or reimbursed by your mutual insurance fund (Belgian social security).

The study does not involve any additional costs. You will not receive any financial compensation for participating in this trial.

Which data are collected about me during the trial and what will happen with them?

1.  Which data are collected and processed during the trial?

The collected and processed personal data concern information about your health and medical condition. This includes your medical history, some of your background information (for example your age, sex, and ethnic origin) and the results of examinations required by the trial.

Ethnic origin data maybe collected to ensure that research data are processed in the broadest possible population.

2.  How will the investigator treat my personal data?

The investigator (and the trial staff) is bound by professional secrecy or by a confidentiality agreement about the data collected.

This means that he/she will never reveal your identity, including in a scientific publication or a lecture and that he/she will encode your data (Ref. [1]) (that is by replacing your identity by an identification code in the trial) before sending them to the sponsor. In other words, this code will not contain any personal identifier or combination of identifiers such as name, first name, initials, full or partial date of birth, file number, etc.

Therefore, the investigator and the trial staff under the responsibility of the investigator, will be the only ones able to establish a link between your identity and the data transmitted during the trial, with the exceptions listed under section 12.5.

The data transmitted to the sponsor will not allow the sponsor to identify you.

3.  How will my data be handled?

Your trial data will be processed in accordance with the current European and Belgian data protection legislation.

The reason that your personal data may be processed for the purposes of the study is that it is processed for the conduct of scientific research and for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest.

4.  Do I have access to my data collected and processed during the trial and can I rectify them?

You are entitled to ask the investigator what data are being collected about you and how those data will be used in connection with the trial.

You have the right

- to inspect and access these data

- to receive the personal data that are collected

- to ask for correction if they are incorrect,

- to restrict the processing of your data.

- to withdraw your consent for the processing of personal data. However personal data collected before withdrawal will be kept to avoid skewing of results in the trial.

It is not possible to have all your data erased to avoid skewing of results in the trial.

1.  Who, other than the Investigator and his trial staff, has access to my personal data?

To verify the quality of the trial, it is possible that your personal uncoded data or information in your medical records relevant for the trial, will be examined by people outside the trial staff but under the responsibility of the investigator. These persons must be subject to professional secrecy or a confidentiality agreement. The following might be considered:

- the personnel designated by the sponsor of the trial (monitors and auditors), and people or organisations providing services for or collaborating with the sponsor. They will however never transfer your name and contact details to the sponsor.

- inspectors of competent health authorities worldwide

- an independent audit group

- people designated by the Ethics Committee

For the needs of the trial, the encoded trial data may be sent to other EU and non-EU countries and may be reviewed by

- personnel (other than the inspectors) of competent health authorities of Belgium (Federal agency for medicines and health products, FAMHP) and other EU and non-EU countries,

- the evaluating Belgian Ethics Committee(s),

- external researchers,

- the sponsor of the trial, personnel designated by the sponsor, and people or organisations providing services for or collaborating with the sponsor, and/or

- group companies of the sponsor in Belgium, and in other EU and non-EU countries.

The European regulation and the Belgian legislation on data protection have requirements for transferring data to non-EU countries. The sponsor must ensure equivalent guarantees regarding personal data protection standards before transferring the encoded data to non-EU countries. If for this purpose, there is a data protection agreement, a copy of this agreement may be obtained via the investigator. You can always contact your investigator to obtain more information about any such transfers.

1.  What will happen to the results of the trial?

After trial closure, a description and the results of this clinical trial will be published in specialised medical journals. A copy of the scientific publication or a summary for laypersons can be obtained from the investigator or the trial staff.

A description of the trial will also be available on https://euclinicaltrials.eu/search-for-clinical-trials/?lang=en and/or https://www.Clinicaltrials.gov. You can search these websites at any time using the information on the front page of the informed consent form. Within 1 year after the study's completion, the website will contain a summary of the results in accordance with the European Commission's guidelines.

These websites or publications will not include information that can identify you.

2.  Will my data be used for other purposes than for the trial in which I take part?

The results of the trial will be used to answer the scientific questions of the trial. In addition, the sponsor would like to use your data obtained from this trial, in connection with other research and development activities (and the associated scientific publications). These activities can only concern research about the same disease or medication.
